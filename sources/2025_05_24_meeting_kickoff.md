# Meeting 1 — Business & User Discovery Notes

## Discussion Points

* The Regulatory Reporting Manager explained that the team is responsible for preparing and validating regulatory capital reporting for the UK entity and supporting several related regulatory reporting activities. The team is currently preparing for the transition to the Basel 3.1 framework, with the largest operational changes expected around the 1 January 2027 implementation date.

* The manager said that the team spends a significant amount of time answering questions that appear simple but require analysts to look across multiple regulatory sources before they can provide a reliable answer. Examples include determining what a particular reporting field means, whether a requirement applies to the bank, which version of a requirement is relevant, and how a reporting instruction relates to the underlying prudential requirement.

* Analyst 1 described the current process as starting with a question from the reporting team, another internal team, or a subject-matter expert. The analyst usually starts with an existing internal knowledge base or previous analysis, then searches the PRA website and other regulatory material. If the answer is not obvious, they open several documents and search within them for relevant terminology.

* Analyst 1 said that finding the first potentially relevant document is generally not the hardest part. The difficulty is establishing that the document is actually the **right source**, that it is applicable to the question being asked, and that it has not been superseded by a later publication.

* Analyst 2 said that regulatory terminology creates another problem. The same concept can appear in a rule, supervisory statement, reporting instruction, template instruction, policy statement or another related document, but the wording and level of detail can differ. Analysts therefore often follow references from one document to another before they are comfortable answering a question.

* Analyst 2 gave an example of a question concerning a reporting template where the answer could not be obtained confidently from the template instructions alone. The analyst had to look at the relevant reporting rule, then check related definitions and previous regulatory material before confirming what the field represented.

* The Regulatory Change/Reporting SME said that this cross-referencing is particularly important during a regulatory transition. A document may describe the existing requirement while another document describes a future requirement. Analysts need to know whether they are answering a question about the current reporting regime, the future Basel 3.1 regime, or the transition between the two.

* The SME said that effective dates are therefore critical. An answer that is technically correct but refers to a requirement that is not yet applicable can still be operationally wrong.

* The manager said that analysts currently maintain their own collections of useful documents, links, spreadsheets and previous interpretations. There is no single place that contains every piece of information required to answer regulatory questions.

* Analyst 1 said that experienced analysts have an advantage because they remember where particular information is likely to be found. Newer analysts can spend considerably longer searching for the same answer.

* Analyst 2 said that analysts also ask colleagues for help through informal channels when they cannot find an answer. The team frequently relies on people who have worked on similar regulatory changes before.

* The manager said this creates a dependency on individual experience. When an experienced analyst is unavailable, other team members may repeat research that has already been performed elsewhere.

* The SME clarified that previous internal interpretations should not automatically be treated as authoritative. An analyst may use a previous interpretation as a starting point, but the underlying regulatory source needs to be checked before the interpretation is reused.

* The manager said that the team currently spends a lot of time producing evidence for answers. When an analyst responds to a question, they often include links, document references, extracts or screenshots so that another person can independently verify the conclusion.

* Analyst 1 said that this evidence-gathering is valuable but time-consuming. The analyst may spend five minutes understanding the answer and another fifteen or twenty minutes finding the exact section that supports it and presenting the evidence clearly.

* Analyst 2 said that simply returning a regulatory document would not solve the problem. Analysts need help getting from a broad document to the specific section, paragraph, table, reporting instruction or definition relevant to the question.

* The SME emphasised that the distinction between **finding information** and **interpreting information** is important. The team would welcome assistance with finding and organising evidence, but they would not want an AI system to present an uncertain interpretation as a regulatory conclusion.

* The SME said that there are questions where professional judgement is unavoidable. Two analysts may initially interpret a requirement differently and need to discuss the issue with a regulatory SME. An AI assistant should not give the impression that such questions have an objectively determined answer simply because it generated a confident response.

* The manager said that the desired outcome is not to replace the regulatory SME. The team wants analysts to reach the SME with better-prepared questions and stronger evidence when escalation is necessary.

* Analyst 1 said that a useful system would allow an analyst to ask a question in normal language rather than having to know the exact regulatory terminology used in the source document.

* Analyst 2 agreed but said that the system must show **where the answer came from**. They would be uncomfortable using an AI-generated answer if they could not immediately inspect the supporting source.

* The manager said that source references would need to be sufficiently precise. A link to a 300-page policy document would not be enough if the system claimed that a particular statement was supported by that document.

* The SME said that the system should distinguish between different types of regulatory material. For example, a final rule, reporting instruction, supervisory statement and an older consultation document should not all appear to have the same authority.

* Analyst 2 said that another useful capability would be identifying related material automatically. If an analyst searches for a particular reporting requirement, it would be helpful to see related definitions, reporting instructions and relevant regulatory changes without having to manually discover every connection.

* The manager said that the team should be able to use the system for both straightforward questions and more involved research. However, she acknowledged that trying to solve every type of regulatory research problem in the first release would make the product too broad.

* The Regulatory Change/Reporting SME said that the Basel 3.1 implementation creates a particularly large research problem because analysts need to understand not only the final rules but also how reporting requirements, instructions and templates change as part of implementation.

* Analyst 1 said that they would find it useful if the system could answer questions such as "What does this reporting field mean?" and "Where does this requirement come from?" They would also like to ask a question and receive the relevant supporting material without manually searching several regulatory documents.

* Analyst 2 said that another potentially useful question would be "What has changed?" when comparing an existing requirement with a future requirement. However, the manager said that she did not want the team to assume that this capability automatically belongs in the first release.

* The SME agreed that identifying regulatory changes is useful but said that determining the downstream impact of a regulatory change on systems, processes, reporting templates and controls is a much larger problem than simply finding the relevant regulatory text.

* The manager said that the team currently has no consistent way of recording whether an answer produced by previous research was useful, whether it was subsequently corrected, or which source ultimately resolved the question.

* Analyst 1 said that analysts sometimes discover after several weeks that a previous answer was based on an outdated document or incomplete interpretation. There is no systematic feedback mechanism for capturing this information.

* The manager said that she would like any future solution to improve consistency between analysts, particularly for recurring questions, but did not want the team to become dependent on an AI answer without appropriate review.

* Analyst 2 said that speed is important, but accuracy and trust are more important for regulatory work. They would rather spend another minute checking an answer than use a fast answer that cannot be verified.

* The SME said that the team should regard the system as an **assistant for regulatory research**, not as the final authority on regulatory interpretation.

* The manager said that, from her perspective, a successful first version would reduce the time analysts spend searching and assembling evidence while maintaining or improving the quality of the resulting answers.

* The manager also said that adoption would depend on whether experienced analysts believe the system saves them time. If the system produces generic summaries that still require the analyst to perform the original research, the team is unlikely to use it regularly.

* Analyst 1 said that the biggest potential benefit for experienced analysts may therefore be reducing repetitive searching and evidence collection rather than eliminating expert judgement.

* Analyst 2 said that the biggest benefit for less experienced analysts may be helping them understand where to look and giving them a starting point for their research.

* The SME cautioned that these two use cases are related but not identical. A system designed to answer questions for experienced analysts may need to behave differently from one designed to teach newer analysts how to investigate a regulatory issue.

* The manager said that the initial product should probably focus on a relatively narrow regulatory domain rather than attempting to cover every regulatory question handled by the bank.

* When discussing the upcoming Basel 3.1 implementation, the group agreed that capital regulatory reporting provides a realistic initial domain because the team is already spending significant effort understanding the changes and their reporting implications.

* The SME noted that the regulatory material is not static. New clarifications, amendments and related publications can appear after an analyst has already performed research. The product would therefore need to deal with document dates and versions rather than treating the regulatory corpus as a permanently fixed knowledge base.

* Analyst 2 said that they would want to know when the system is uncertain or cannot find adequate evidence. A response such as "I couldn't find sufficient evidence to answer this" would be preferable to a plausible-looking answer without strong support.

* The manager agreed and said that escalation should be treated as a valid outcome rather than a product failure.

* The group discussed whether the system should automatically determine whether a requirement applies to a particular reporting entity. The SME said that this could be useful in some circumstances but warned that applicability can depend on firm characteristics, permissions, consolidation structure and other context. The group did not agree that this should be treated as an automatic decision in the first version.

* The manager said that the system should not directly submit regulatory returns, change production reporting systems, or make decisions that become part of the bank's regulatory reporting process without human review.

* Analyst 1 said that, even if the system eventually became capable of more advanced tasks, they would still want the initial version to make it obvious that the analyst remains responsible for reviewing the result.

* The manager summarised the main problem as the amount of **time and effort required to find, connect, validate and present regulatory information**, rather than a lack of access to regulatory documents themselves.

* The SME added that the corresponding risk is not simply wasted time. An incomplete search, outdated source or misunderstood requirement can lead to an incorrect interpretation being circulated internally.

* The group agreed that any useful solution would need to make the underlying regulatory evidence easy to inspect and would need to handle uncertainty explicitly.

* The manager said that there are several possible product directions emerging from the discussion, including regulatory search, question answering, evidence gathering, document comparison and regulatory-change analysis. She did not want the team to assume that all of these belong in the first product release.

* The manager asked that the next stage of discovery distinguish between capabilities that solve the **core research problem** and capabilities that are attractive extensions of it.

* The group agreed that the initial product should be considered an internal tool for regulatory-reporting professionals, with the initial scope centred on UK capital regulatory reporting and the Basel 3.1 transition.

* No decision was made during the meeting on the final MVP feature set, technical architecture, model, retrieval approach, evaluation methodology, implementation timeline or success metrics.

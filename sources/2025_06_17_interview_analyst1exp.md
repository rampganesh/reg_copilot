# Analyst 1 — Experienced Regulatory Reporting Analyst

## Opening

The analyst was told that the discussion was focused on recent real work rather than opinions about a proposed product, and that examples of difficult or frustrating cases were particularly useful.

## Module A — Workflow Walkthrough

### 1. Last regulatory question answered end-to-end

The analyst described a recent question about how a particular capital-reporting requirement should be treated under the changing Basel 3.1 regime.

The question initially came through as a relatively simple request, but answering it involved:

* Identifying the relevant PRA material.
* Checking whether the source was current or related to the future Basel 3.1 framework.
* Following references from the initial document to related rules and reporting instructions.
* Checking whether the reporting treatment was described elsewhere in more specific reporting material.
* Verifying the interpretation with existing internal knowledge/context.
* Preparing a response that included enough evidence for the recipient to validate the conclusion.

The final response contained the conclusion, supporting regulatory references, and enough context for the recipient to understand why those sources were relevant.

The analyst said the largest amount of effort was **not necessarily finding the first relevant document**. More time was spent confirming that it was the correct version and source, following cross-references, and assembling defensible evidence for the final response.

### 2. What happened when the first document looked relevant?

The analyst said the first search result was treated as a starting point rather than proof that the correct answer had been found.

They checked:

* What type of document it was.
* Whether it was final, proposed or historical.
* Its effective date.
* Whether a more specific reporting instruction or related rule existed.
* Whether references within the document pointed to another authoritative source.

The analyst recalled instances where an apparently relevant document turned out not to be the appropriate source because it was older, proposed, or answered a slightly different aspect of the question.

The main lesson was that **source validation is a normal part of the work, not an exceptional check**.

## Module B — Trust & Provenance

### 3. A time when research was not trusted or was set aside

The analyst described rejecting research when the supporting evidence was too vague to independently verify.

Examples included answers that:

* Cited only a broad document without identifying the relevant section.
* Relied on an interpretation without showing the underlying regulatory wording.
* Used an apparently relevant source whose status or date was unclear.
* Presented a confident conclusion even though related sources appeared to point in different directions.

The fastest way to establish confidence was to show the **specific authoritative source and the precise passage or reporting instruction supporting the conclusion**, together with enough context to understand its applicability.

The analyst emphasised that a citation is useful only if it reduces the effort required to verify the answer.

### 4. When someone asked to see the working

The analyst said that when challenged on a regulatory interpretation, they normally provided the relevant source document and a precise reference to the applicable section or instruction.

For more complicated questions, they would also provide the chain of supporting sources or explain how one provision related to another.

A document title alone was considered insufficient for a material regulatory conclusion. For reporting requirements, the useful reference could be more granular — for example, a particular table, reporting instruction, field or template reference.

## Module C — Uncertainty & Escalation

### 5. A question they could not answer confidently

The analyst described situations where the available material did not provide enough evidence to confidently determine the answer, particularly where:

* Multiple regulatory sources needed to be reconciled.
* The applicability depended on circumstances not contained in the question.
* The material was changing or had different effective dates.
* A source appeared relevant but its status or relationship to another document was unclear.

Rather than forcing a conclusion, the analyst escalated to a regulatory/reporting SME or asked the requester for additional context.

The requester generally accepted this when the analyst could explain **what was uncertain and what needed to be resolved**.

The analyst distinguished between saying “I don't know” and saying “these are the relevant sources, but this particular point requires SME confirmation.”

### 6. Discovering later that an answer used outdated material

The analyst recalled that outdated material can become an issue particularly during regulatory transition periods.

When an earlier answer is discovered to have relied on superseded material, the concern is not only correcting the answer. There is also a need to understand:

* Who received the original interpretation.
* Whether anyone acted on it.
* What the replacement requirement is.
* Whether other related questions may have been answered using the same interpretation.

The analyst said this creates a strong reason for preserving the source and date associated with an answer rather than treating answers as timeless pieces of knowledge.

## Module D — Divergent Value Propositions

### 7. Helping a newer colleague find something

The analyst said they generally showed newer colleagues **how to approach the research**, rather than simply giving them the answer.

They would explain:

* Where to start looking.
* Which regulatory sources tend to be authoritative for the question.
* How to recognise whether material is current or superseded.
* Which terminology or references to follow.
* How to validate the final evidence.

The analyst said this is different from how they personally work. Experience means they can often jump directly to likely sources or recognise relevant terminology without consciously following every step.

When they were newer, they relied much more heavily on colleagues, previous examples and knowing which documents contained particular types of information.

### 8. What would they not hand to a tool?

The analyst would not hand over final responsibility for:

* Determining regulatory applicability where judgement is required.
* Making a definitive compliance interpretation without human review.
* Resolving genuinely conflicting regulatory evidence silently.
* Making decisions where important contextual information is missing.
* Producing a final answer that could be acted upon without someone checking the evidence.

They were comfortable with a tool helping with **search, navigation, cross-referencing and initial interpretation**, provided the underlying evidence remained visible.

## Module E — Evaluation

### 9. What would prove the tool works?

The analyst said they would want to test it against **realistic historical regulatory questions**, particularly difficult ones rather than straightforward searches.

They would expect the tool to:

* Find the genuinely relevant sources.
* Distinguish current, future and superseded material.
* Provide precise and accurate citations.
* Avoid inventing regulatory wording or references.
* Recognise when evidence is insufficient.
* Surface relevant related material without overwhelming the user.
* Save meaningful time compared with the existing process.

They said the tool would lose trust quickly if it produced a confident answer with an impressive-looking but incorrect citation.

The strongest evidence of value would therefore be **faster research without sacrificing the analyst's ability to verify the conclusion**.

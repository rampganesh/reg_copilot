# Analyst 2 — Provenance-Focused Regulatory Reporting Analyst

## Opening

The analyst was told that the interview was about recent real work and that the objective was to understand actual behaviour, particularly around evidence, verification and difficult cases.

## Module A — Workflow Walkthrough

### 1. Last regulatory question answered end-to-end

The analyst described a recent request requiring clarification of a reporting treatment. They began with a targeted search using the terminology from the request, then opened several potentially relevant regulatory sources.

The analyst did not rely on the first useful-looking result. They checked the source's:

* Regulatory status.
* Publication/version information.
* Effective date.
* Relationship to other documents.
* Specific reporting relevance.

They then followed cross-references and checked the reporting instructions/templates where appropriate.

The final response was deliberately structured around the evidence. The analyst included precise references so that another person could reproduce the research and check the conclusion.

The analyst said that **assembling and validating provenance was one of the most time-consuming parts**, particularly when several documents contributed to the answer.

### 2. How the first relevant document was validated

The analyst described a fairly strict validation process.

Finding a document containing the right terminology was not enough. They wanted to establish:

1. Is this an authoritative source?
2. Is it the correct version?
3. Is it operative, future-effective, proposed or historical?
4. Does it actually answer the question?
5. Is there a more specific reporting instruction?
6. Are there linked provisions that change the interpretation?

They recalled cases where a document appeared to answer the question but another source contained the more specific reporting requirement.

The analyst therefore viewed **search relevance and regulatory correctness as two different things**.

## Module B — Trust & Provenance

### 3. When research was not trusted

The analyst said they had set aside research where the conclusion could not be traced cleanly back to authoritative evidence.

Particularly problematic were:

* Broad document-level citations.
* Screenshots without sufficient context.
* References to “PRA guidance” without a precise location.
* Answers based on secondary explanations when primary material was available.
* Citations that could not be reproduced.
* Statements that appeared inconsistent with the status or effective date of the cited document.

They said the quickest way to trust an answer was to be able to **click or navigate directly to the exact supporting provision and verify that the source actually said what the answer claimed**.

### 4. When someone asked to see the working

The analyst said they normally provided the source document plus a granular reference.

For straightforward textual requirements, a section or paragraph reference could be enough. For reporting questions, they might need to identify a specific:

* Reporting template.
* Table.
* Row/field.
* Reporting instruction.
* Instructional paragraph.

For complicated conclusions, they would provide multiple references and explain how they connected.

The analyst considered provenance part of the answer itself, rather than supporting material that could be added later.

## Module C — Uncertainty & Escalation

### 5. A question they could not answer confidently

The analyst described a case where the available sources did not support a single clear conclusion.

Instead of selecting the most plausible interpretation, they documented the relevant evidence and escalated the unresolved point to the appropriate SME.

They said that escalation is most useful when the analyst can provide a **well-bounded question with the relevant evidence already assembled**.

An unstructured escalation creates more work for the SME; a provenance-backed escalation allows the SME to focus on the judgement that actually requires expertise.

### 6. Discovering an outdated answer weeks later

The analyst said that discovering an answer was based on outdated material would raise two separate concerns:

* Whether the original answer needs to be corrected.
* Whether the same outdated interpretation may have influenced other work.

They felt it was important to retain the **temporal context of the original answer**. Replacing an old answer with a new one without preserving what the original answer was based on would make later investigation difficult.

They also felt that simply labelling a source “latest” would be inadequate because a newly published document is not necessarily the currently applicable requirement.

## Module D — Divergent Value Propositions

### 7. Helping a newer colleague

The analyst said they would normally teach a newer colleague the research path:

* Which source types to search.
* How to distinguish authoritative material from supporting material.
* How to check dates and status.
* How to trace references.
* How to document evidence.

They said newer colleagues often need help understanding **why one source should be trusted over another**, whereas an experienced analyst may recognise this from experience.

Previous examples and colleagues were particularly important sources of practical knowledge when they were newer.

### 8. What would they not hand to a tool?

The analyst would not delegate the final judgement where:

* Applicability is uncertain.
* Sources conflict.
* Material is ambiguous.
* Important contextual facts are missing.
* The conclusion could have significant regulatory or reporting consequences.

They would be comfortable using automation to reduce mechanical research effort, including searching, document discovery, cross-referencing and evidence assembly.

However, they stressed that **the tool should expose uncertainty rather than hide it behind a confident answer**.

## Module E — Evaluation

### 9. What would prove the tool works?

The analyst said provenance quality would be a major part of their evaluation.

They would test whether the system:

* Retrieves the correct source rather than merely a semantically similar one.
* Identifies the correct version/status.
* Cites the exact supporting provision.
* Preserves enough surrounding context.
* Does not fabricate citations or quotations.
* Correctly handles tables and reporting instructions.
* Identifies conflicting or insufficient evidence.
* Makes it faster to reproduce and validate the research.

They would deliberately include difficult evaluation cases where:

* The most obvious document is not the correct source.
* Current and future requirements coexist.
* Multiple documents need to be connected.
* A source has been superseded.
* The answer cannot safely be determined from the available evidence.

For this analyst, **a fast answer with weak provenance would not constitute success**. The tool would need to demonstrate that it can reduce research effort while maintaining or improving the auditability and reproducibility of the analyst's work.
